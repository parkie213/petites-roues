# Verbs followed by an infinitive

## Only the first verb is conjugated

When one verb leads into another, French conjugates the first and leaves the second in the
infinitive, the dictionary form ending in `-er`, `-ir` or `-re`.

- J'adore **sauter** dans les flaques. — I love jumping in puddles.
- Tu dois **mettre** tes bottes. — You must put on your boots.
- Est-ce qu'on peut **sortir jouer** ? — Can we go outside and play?

The last one chains two: `sortir jouer` is "go out to play", with no word for "to" or "and".

## English -ing and "to" are both an infinitive

English switches between "love jumping" and "want to jump"; French uses the infinitive for
both.

| English | French |
|---|---|
| I love jumping | j'**adore sauter** |
| I like jumping | j'**aime bien sauter** |
| I want to jump | je **veux sauter** |
| I can jump | je **peux sauter** |

`Aimer bien` is softer than `aimer`: with things and activities it means "like", while `adorer`
is "love".

## Some verbs need `de`

A few verbs and expressions link to the infinitive with `de`, which has no English
equivalent here:

- Si tu as **envie de** sauter… — If you want to jump… (`avoir envie de`: feel like, want)
- J'ai **oublié de** mettre mes bottes. — I forgot to put on my boots.

Learn the `de` with the verb. `S'occuper de` takes a noun in the clip: Peppa aime bien
**s'occuper de** son petit frère — Peppa likes looking after her little brother.

## Negation wraps the first verb

`Ne … pas` goes around the conjugated verb, and the infinitive follows:

- Je **ne** peux **pas** sortir. — I can't go out.

Putting both words before the infinitive changes the meaning: je veux **ne pas** sortir — I
want not to go out (I'd rather stay in).
